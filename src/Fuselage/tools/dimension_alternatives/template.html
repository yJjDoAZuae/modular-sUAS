What each scheme actually answers

Three questions, and what each scheme spends on them
 

Scheme A is not a near miss, it is a different document. R2.05 and a 4.86 mm pocket are both true and both inspectable, and there is no route from either to “4.00 mm longeron, 0.10 clearance”. The clearance is the term that would let a reader work backwards and scheme A never prints it. It answers an inspector completely and an integrator not at all.

Scheme B fails the opposite way, and more quietly. Every hardware size is right there — and a caliper on the bore reads 4.10 against a drawing that says 4.00, with nothing on the sheet to explain the difference. A drawing whose every dimension disagrees with the part is worse than one that omits them.

So the choice is between C and D, and it is about where the hardware lives, not whether it appears. C puts it in a callout beside the dimension; D moves it into a schedule and tags the dimension. C costs nothing off the view. D costs table rows — and five of the thirteen family sheets already measure 19 rows of 19 available, with nothing spare.

The aperture, and what it is not

Decided 2026‑08‑22: the span between the interior faces is a dimension and belongs on the drawings. It is a distance between two real parallel faces, so it dimensions like anything else.

It is an expression, not a measurement, which is what makes it generable: unit_width − 2·(panel_thickness + panel_tolerance) − 2·flange_thickness = {{aperture_w}}. The boom bulkhead uses web_width in place of flange_thickness and comes to 78.275, because its inner boundary is set by the web rather than the flange.

On a boom bulkhead the span and the clear opening differ. The span is 78.275 both ways; the clear opening is 78.275 × 66.402, because the boom collet intrudes from one side. The dimension is the span and the obstruction is visible in the view — but this is the one part where “what fits through” is not the number on the drawing.

The panel allocation

Panels are to get their own OML allocation, with shapes designed inside it so designs can be interchanged. The frame determines every boundary of it, and the bulkhead section is the one view that can carry them — because its outer profile is the panel’s seating surface.

The width is confirmed by a clearance rather than by a face, which is the stronger check. Take the panel to be {{panel_width}} wide and its edge lands at corner‑local x = −2.5000, against a slot bottom at −2.4000. The gap is 0.1000 — panel_tolerance exactly. No other width fits the slot the corner was actually cut with.

The panel runs the full bay, and the bulkhead is cut out to let it. The geometry says so twice. The bulkhead’s outer profile sits 4.8625 mm inboard of the mold line — exactly the panel pocket — and one of its outer flat faces measures 392.850 mm², which is 65.475 × 6: the panel’s exposed span times the bulkhead thickness. The bulkhead’s outer flat face is the panel’s seating surface, cut back by precisely the panel it seats and running precisely as far. So a panel is not interrupted at a station; the bulkhead gives way to it rather than the other way round.

What is left open is what owns the envelope. Interchange needs something a panel designer can be handed and a check can read against, and today it exists only as these expressions inside a generator.

The assembly view

Required 2026‑08‑22: the panel and the corner projected onto the bulkhead top view, with the panel width and thickness as dimensions and the two clearances as callouts.

The bulkhead top view is the carrier because it is the only view where all three parts share a plane. The bulkhead’s outer flat face is the panel’s seating surface, and the corner’s panel extension ends at 32.7375 mm from the airframe axis — a position the bulkhead carries a face at as well. Nothing here is constructed onto the view; the parts are where they are.

The corner tolerance callout reads 0.00, and that is the reason to put it there. OQ‑DES‑C5 held the parameter at 0 and wrote down the residual: if these faces are bonded, 0 leaves no bond line. Printing it puts a live question in front of whoever assembles the airframe. This is not the structural‑zero case the scheme warns about — that rule is for a joint the part does not have, and this joint exists with a clearance somebody chose.

It also needs both parts’ parameter tables. corner_tolerance is not in the bulkhead’s table at all, because the corner carries the whole of that clearance and the bulkhead passes zero. Needing two tables is what makes this an assembly drawing rather than two part drawings on one sheet.

Material, in a data block

Not a dimension and not a callout: a callout points at a feature, and volume is a property of the whole part.

It is also not a property of the design. The corner does not change shape to suit a slicing choice the way the cowl does — the cowl’s wall thickness reaches its geometry, and the corner is the same solid at one perimeter or five. So a sheet stating a mass would state something the design does not determine. What it carries instead is the two numbers that let a reader work out material for their own settings, neither of which depends on a slicing choice. Solid volume overstates a two‑perimeter corner by about 1.8×, less than it looks like it should, because the corner’s section is chunky: 145 mm² of area behind only about 55 mm of perimeter. The bulkhead is nearly solid at two perimeters because it is already a thin ring. The estimate is wall area × perimeters × 0.6 mm, capped at the solid volume where the part is thinner than two walls and the two walls are the same material.

Mass stays off the sheet. It needs a filament density and the reader’s perimeter count, and the design constrains neither.

What the runner-up would have looked like

Scheme D lost on sheet space, not on merit: five of the thirteen family sheets already measure 19 table rows of 19 available. Its schedule is worth recording because it becomes the answer if scheme C’s three‑line callouts turn out not to place — which is measurable and has not been measured.

The bolt hole is the row worth looking at twice. It is Ø{{bolt_bare}} for a nominal 4 mm bolt — a line‑to‑line fit with no clearance at all, because joint 10 is the one interface in the register with no clearance parameter. The anchor variant bores Ø5.50 for a heat‑set insert instead. No scheme here fixes that; what B, C and D do is make it visible, by printing the fastener size beside the hole size. Scheme A prints Ø{{bolt_bare}} and says nothing, and a reader has no way to notice that the bolt will not pass.
